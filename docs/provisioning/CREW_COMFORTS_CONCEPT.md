# Alcohol and the crew's comforts — concept notes

**Status: concept, companion to `docs/provisioning/PROVISIONING_CONCEPT.md`.**
Written 2026-08-14 from Ash's second brief (the role of alcohol; drunkenness as
a crew mechanic; singing, gambling, money, and "what else have I overlooked")
plus two further research passes (sources §8). Same contract as the parent
document: research plus design thinking, no implementation implied, Ash owns
every call. Where a practice is real but *later* than our 1780s–1800s window
it is dated and flagged — this document is as much about what to keep **off**
the ship as what to put on it.

---

## 1. Ash's questions, answered short

- **How significant was alcohol?** Central — structurally central, not
  incidental. A Royal Navy seaman's spirit ration was **~12–13 modern units of
  alcohol a day**; the day's two grog issues were its emotional high points
  ("around this point of time concentrate half the meditations of the day" —
  a ship's chaplain, 1829); and drink was cited in **~30% of all formal
  punishments** (5,000-event log study, 1783–1815). Admirals institutionally
  *assumed* afternoon impairment: St Vincent banned punishing men after the
  evening grog because they were no longer answerable for themselves.
- **Did they drink all day or was it controlled?** In the navy: tightly
  ritualized, and **the ceremony was the control** — two supervised issues
  (with the noon dinner and at the late-afternoon watch change; not
  sundown-gated), mixed 3:1–4:1 with water at a tub on deck under the
  lieutenant's eye, in the American service *drunk on the spot* at the tub.
  Selling or giving your ration away was punishable — **both giver and
  taker**. Beer stations were different: a gallon a day of 2–3% small beer
  sipped through the day, closer to liquid bread than drink.
- **On a ship like ours?** The merchant service — our hull's lineage — had
  **no statutory ration at all**: drams at the master's discretion, roughly
  *half* the navy scale by the one small-schooner manifest found, and issued
  by *event* (after reefing, in cold, on feast days) rather than by clock.
  The captain **is** the rule — which for a game where the player is the
  captain is the whole design (§6.1).
- **Beer barrels or spirits?** Spirits. Beer soured in weeks and was
  effectively a home-waters drink (ration abolished 1831); every expedition
  precedent victualled spirits plus wine. The parent doc's "no beer gauge"
  refusal stands, now with evidence. The period-correct "beer moment" is
  **spruce beer brewed at landfalls** — Cook did it at every wooding stop.
- **Alcoholism?** Recognized, named, and studied *from the fleet*: repeat
  offenders are visible in every punishment log (one man: four floggings in
  two years, all for drunkenness, then discharged); delirium tremens was a
  returnable disease category ("the horrors"); and naval surgeon **Thomas
  Trotter's 1804 *Essay on Drunkenness* — the first book-length disease model
  of addiction — came directly out of twenty years' naval practice.** The
  period's own data shows the lash demonstrably did not cure the habitual
  drunkard. That is a mechanic, not just a fact (§6.2).
- **A drunkenness level affecting how crew operate?** Yes — and it lands on
  machinery we already own: the S5 human-operator model gives every crew
  action perception noise and response delays; intoxication is a widening of
  those exact parameters, with a built-in daily rhythm (sober forenoon,
  merry afternoon) that the historical record hands us for free.
- **Singing?** Not shanties — **shanties are an anachronism** for us
  (post-1815 merchant practice; the word is 1850s). Period-correct: wordless
  "singing out" at the windlass, work done to a **fiddle or fife**, and
  off-watch songs and **dancing in the dog watches** — which Cook ordered as
  exercise and Bligh made compulsory. Big art-direction consequence (§6.5).
- **Gambling and money?** Cash barely existed at sea (pay in arrears at
  voyage's end), so **grog and tobacco were the ship's true currencies**.
  Gambling was banned at the captain's discretion and *never appears in
  punishment logs* — it surfaced as the fights and thefts it caused, which is
  exactly how a game should model it (§6.4).
- **Overlooked?** Pets as crew, the ceremonies calendar (crossing the line
  ran on rum forfeits; Christmas drunkenness was *licensed*, and Cook
  shortened sail beforehand in case weather came on while all hands
  caroused), mail and speaking ships, wonder (bioluminescence is all over the
  period journals — and we own an ocean renderer), and the medical reality of
  homesickness: "nostalgia" was a diagnosable, occasionally fatal condition.

## 2. Alcohol: how it actually worked

### 2.1 The dose

- RN spirit ration to 1824: ½ pint of rum at ~54.5% ABV (contract strength,
  fixed from 1784) — **~12–13 units/day**. The beer gallon: ~6–9 units. Wine
  pint: ~6. So a crew's ethanol intake *doubled* when it left home waters for
  a spirits station — and the punishment statistics move with it (drunkenness
  ~3% of punishments in home waters vs ~20% overseas in the Seven Years' War
  data).
- Substitution was the master equivalence: 1 gal beer = 1 pint wine = ½ pint
  spirits (Regulations, 1731). Allowances were substitutes, not additive.
- Officers drank separately and better (the wardroom's "blackstrap", the
  captain's Madeira and port), **and the rules against buying liquor did not
  bind the quarterdeck — the lower deck resented the asymmetry.** A captain
  seen drunk while flogging drunkards was a known figure of grievance.

### 2.2 The ceremony was the control

Vernon's 1740 grog order (the founding text — rum drunk neat in drams was
"attended with many fatal effects to their morals as well as their health")
built a control system whose parts are all *visible, stageable actions*:

- mixed in a scuttled butt **on deck, in the presence of the lieutenant of
  the watch**, "to see that the men are not defrauded";
- two issues, noon and late afternoon; names called, crossed off;
- dilution 3:1 by the 1790s (memoirists agree), often with sugar and lemon;
- no sale, no gift — St Vincent punished both parties;
- boys' grog stopped (1806) because it was visibly flowing to older men;
- the spirit room below, physically secured, spirits *pumped* up at seven
  bells rather than anyone carrying an open light among the casks.

Against it ran a genuine smuggling war: bottles in boats' water kegs, skins
moored underwater to the anchor buoy, **"sucking the monkey"** (coconuts
refilled with rum by bumboat women, from the 1770s), bladders under
clothing; a smuggled bottle could fetch months of a man's pay. Some captains
licensed controlled amounts instead — Riou allowed liquor "for those messes
who know how to make proper use of it." **The binge risk lived in port and
at anchor, not at sea**: crews paid immediately before sailing were
notoriously drunk for days after weighing.

### 2.3 Drunkenness and discipline, quantified

- Drink cited in **~30%** of summary punishments 1783–1815 — big, but *not*
  the largest category (disobedience/neglect leads at ~39%).
- The going tariff was legible to everyone: "being quietly drunk meant a
  dozen lashes, and many men thought it was worth it." Drunk **at quarters /
  on duty** was a separate, aggravated charge. Peaceable off-watch
  drunkenness was often simply ignored (Rodger: "no man who was peaceably
  drunk would normally be punished for it").
- **Stopping or watering the grog as the standard minor punishment is a
  mid-19th-century development** — in the Georgian navy the answer was the
  cat, and grog-stoppage appears exactly twice in a 5,000-event sample. Note
  for our fiction: Cook docked grog for *dirtiness*, and Bligh stopped the
  grog of two men **for refusing to dance** — captains used the ration as an
  instrument well before the institution did.
- Dilution was felt as theft: the crew of the *Defiance* mutinied in 1794
  over **five-water grog** when 3:1 was the custom.
- The afternoon was institutionally different from the forenoon: Keith
  "noticed a great difference in the men between the morning and the
  afternoon"; St Vincent forbade punishment after evening grog. **The ship
  had a sobriety curve, and everyone navigated by it.**

### 2.4 The period knew the drunkard

Log studies show the repeat-offender profile clearly (John Mahoney, frigate
*Daphne*: four floggings, 72 lashes, two years, every charge "drunkenness and
neglect", finally discharged). Trotter's 1804 essay named the thing: "the
habit of drunkenness is a disease of the mind" — an addiction model thirty
years before the temperance movement, drawn from fleet practice, with the
implicit clinical observation the logs confirm: **punishment did not fix it.**
The shore end of the cycle was described by the *Dreadnought* hospital
physician (1842): crews paid off after long voyages were "kept in a state of
constant intoxication for a fortnight or three weeks" by the lodging-house
economy — sea-time enforced rationing, shore-time delivered the binge.

### 2.5 What a vessel like ours did

- The one quantified small-schooner manifest (whaler *Lydia*, 1765, ~13
  hands, ~5 months): 2 barrels of rum ≈ **130 ml per man-day — half the navy
  ration**, master-issued.
- Dana (merchant brig, 1834): grog served "every middle and morning watch,
  and after every reefing of topsails" off Cape Horn — **issue tied to duty
  events, not the clock**. Ad-lib drinking at sea was not normal on a working
  merchantman; the master controlled the cask.
- Expedition captains used the ration as a management dial: Cook's morning
  brandy drams in Antarctic waters "whenever I thought it necessary";
  Vancouver's extra grog for detached survey-boat crews; Bligh in the launch
  issuing **a teaspoonful of rum a day** — the ritual outlived the food.
- Temperance ships are real but later (documented from the early 1830s; Dana
  already sneering that "the temperance was all in the forecastle … the
  captain can have his brandy"). For a 1790s fiction, a *dry ship* is an
  eccentric captain's choice, not a movement.

### 2.6 Beer, settled

Cook's *Endeavour* carried 1,200 gal of beer and 1,600 gal of spirits plus
3,000 gal of Madeira wine taken en route; the beer was gone in weeks. Small
beer soured fast, was "swipes" at 2–3%, and was formally a harbour drink for
this entire period. Our spirit locker carries rum (issue) and the pantry a
few dozen of the captain's bottled porter and Madeira (set dressing, private
property). Spruce beer at landfalls is the period beer experience — brewed,
drunk fresh, gone.

## 3. Music, play and the informal economy

### 3.1 Music — what is period-correct (and what is not)

- **No shanties.** Call-and-response work songs are post-1815 merchant
  practice; the word itself is 1850s. The RN worked in silence or to a
  **fiddle/fife at the capstan** and discouraged singing at work. What a
  1790s crew does: rhythmic wordless **"singing out"** at the windlass and
  halyards (Falconer 1780: "a sort of song or howl"), and real songs
  **off watch** — mostly the popular songs of the day, not sea songs
  (a sailor-POW's 1777 songbook: 6 of 57 maritime).
- Instruments aboard: fiddle, fife, flute, jew's harp (a fife was excavated
  from a 1785 Whitby collier wreck — our exact hull class). **No concertina,
  no accordion** (1829+, common decades later).
- Ships recruited music deliberately: Cook's *Endeavour* carried a
  drummer-fiddler; **Bligh rated a nearly blind fiddler AB on *Bounty*
  purely to play for the crew.**
- Dancing in the dog watches is the era's exercise-and-morale institution:
  Cook made crews dance to the violin for health; Bligh made 4–8 pm
  "amusement and dancing" compulsory and punished refusal. Hornpipes are
  period; by 1829 "a sailor who could not dance a hornpipe was no sailor."

### 3.2 Games and gambling

- Attested: cards and dice generally, draughts, backgammon, simple dice
  games; and the gloriously period **"able whackets"** (Grose's slang
  dictionary, 1785): loser beaten across the palm with a twisted
  handkerchief — a gambling game whose stake is *pain*, evolved precisely
  because money was scarce and gaming banned. (Crown and anchor as a staple
  is probably later; keep it rare.)
- The Articles of War never mention gambling; bans lived in captains' order
  books. And the punishment logs of three ships across seven years **never
  once record gaming as an offence** — while recording the quarrelling,
  fighting and theft it produced. Gambling was invisible until it wasn't.
- Stakes: grog, tobacco, clothing, future pay. Gambling debt was a known
  engine of messdeck conflict.

### 3.3 Money and the internal economy

- Cash scarcely circulated at sea: RN pay came in arrears at pay-off (pay
  *tickets* functioned as discounted paper ashore); merchant crews took a
  month's advance, typically drunk before sailing (the later "dead horse"
  ceremony marks working it off). **Aboard, the liquid currencies were grog
  and tobacco.** Everything else — slops, soap, tobacco itself — moved
  through the purser's/master's book against wages, settled at voyage's end.
- **The dead man's auction is verified and belongs in the game**: a dead
  shipmate's kit auctioned before the mast, sums entered against bidders'
  wages, proceeds to the estate/widow — with men deliberately overbidding as
  disguised charity, and the superstition that no one wears a dead man's
  clothes on the same voyage.
- **Private ventures**: the merchant sailor's traditional right to carry a
  small trade parcel of his own. For an ex-merchant schooner this is lineage:
  every hand may have a little venture in the hold — a period-native hook
  for port gameplay that isn't a commerce system (and doesn't touch the
  spec's "no trade goods" rule, which is about the *ship's* cargo).
- The **slush fund** already exists in the parent doc (cook's perquisite →
  crew luxuries); it is the crew's one collective purse.

## 4. The morale calendar

Breaks in routine were engineered, licensed, and largely ran on the spirit
ration:

- **Crossing the line** (Banks's journal, 1768): everyone who couldn't prove
  a prior crossing chose between ducking three times from the yard or a
  forfeit of **four days' rum allowance**; even Cook paid up; the dogs were
  on the list. The crew's own court, officers consenting.
- **Christmas**: licensed anarchy — "all hands got abominably drunk … the
  lord knows what would have become of us" (Banks, 1768); Cook **took in
  sail beforehand** so the ship could look after herself while the crew
  caroused, and at anchor sent them ashore afterwards to sober up. The
  cautionary sequel is *Beagle*, day two of her famous voyage: FitzRoy
  flogged several men "for offences brought on by the indulgence granted
  them on Christmas day."
- King's birthday and anniversaries kept with dinners and toasts; the
  **Saturday night toast to "sweethearts and wives"** is period-attested
  (Dibdin, 1789 — treat the full weekday toast rota as later garnish);
  "splice the mainbrace" as *practice* (extra ration after hard duty or
  victory) is solid, the catchphrase's dating is not.
- **Mail and speaking ships**: hailing a homeward-bound ship and passing
  letters was a major emotional event (the whalers' hove-to "gam" is a later
  institution — keep the practice, not the word).
- Sunday rest and the Thursday make-and-mend half-holiday are already in the
  parent doc's weekly rhythm; they are the ordinary tick of the same clock.
- **Pets were crew**: Flinders's cat Trim (shipwreck survivor, got a written
  eulogy); the goat that circumnavigated twice and was pensioned with a
  Latin collar inscription by Dr Johnson. A cat aboard is period, ratting,
  and free characterization.
- **Wonder is documented morale**: period journals record bioluminescence,
  whales, waterspouts with open delight. We already render the ocean at
  night; a milky-sea or sparking wake event is atmosphere the period
  explicitly hands us.

And the dark side of the same calendar: **"nostalgia" was a medical
diagnosis** (dejection, anorexia, sometimes death) that merged at sea with
*calenture* (the delirium in which the sea looks like green fields) and with
scurvy's notorious weeping-at-the-smell-of-land; the doldrums are named for
the mental state the calm belt produced. Long-voyage melancholy is
period-real and gives the surgeon-naturalist something to *do*.

Conflict regulation, briefly, because it frames every mechanic above:
fistfights were floggable but mostly handled out of sight; **grumbling
("growling") was the licensed safety valve**; the line was crossed when
grievance became *combination* — collective, duty-refusing. Spithead 1797 is
the type case: respectful petitions first, order maintained even in mutiny.
A game morale system should know that gradient: growl → deputation →
refusal — with the captain able to intervene at each step (Bainbridge
explaining the water cut turned a near-mutiny into grumbling; that's the
mechanic).

## 5. Design: how this becomes game

### 5.1 The captain's custom — alcohol policy as identity

Because our vessel is ex-merchant with a crew of eight, **there is no
inherited institution: the player writes the ship's drinking custom.** That
is a richer dial than any navy simulation would give us:

| Custom | Historical model | Consequences |
|---|---|---|
| Clock issue, navy-style | RN twice-daily grog tub | steady comfort, steady afternoon impairment, ritual the crew treasures |
| Drams at discretion | merchant/whaler practice (half-ration, event-issued: after reefing, in cold, at feasts) | spirits last longer; issue becomes *reward*, tighter coupling to hardship; less daily ritual |
| Dry ship | 1830s temperance ships (anachronistic-eccentric for us; Dana's sneer: "the temperance was all in the forecastle") | morale floor drops, discipline events drop, crew recruitment/retention flavor |

Sub-dials that are all period-attested: dilution ratio (**weaker grog reads
as theft** — the *Defiance* mutinied over it), extra issues as reward
(mainbrace practice), stoppage as punishment (rare-but-real Georgian;
Cook docked grog for dirtiness), and the licensed blow-out (Christmas,
crossing the line, homeward turn) with its attendant captain's preparations —
**"shorten sail before Christmas" is a real period decision that is also
literally a game verb we already have.**

### 5.2 Drunkenness on the S5 operator

The S5 human-crew model already gives every order a perception, processing
and motor-delay pipeline with noise. Intoxication is a **state that widens
those parameters** — no new machinery, just a modifier with:

- a **daily shape**: sober forenoon, merry afternoon (the historically
  assumed sobriety curve — St Vincent's rule); pronounced under clock-issue
  custom, flattened under drams-at-discretion;
- **context severity**: the same state is comfort off watch, hazard at the
  helm, and an aggravated offence at quarters — matching the period's
  on-duty/off-duty distinction (peaceable off-watch drunkenness was ignored);
- **event spikes**: feast days, smuggled or hoarded stock, port;
- and later, in the crew round, **per-person propensity**: the documented
  habitual-drunkard profile, with the period's own hard finding built in —
  *punishment does not fix him*. Repeated discipline of the same man erodes
  crew temper without changing his behavior; the historical resolutions were
  discharge, tolerance, or tragedy. That is a character arc, not a stat.

Accident coupling (falls from aloft, overboard at night) is period-plausible
but historians have never robustly quantified it — flag: tune freely, cite
nothing.

### 5.3 Morale is five channels, not one bar

The research keeps sorting morale into the same recurring channels; a future
morale design should inherit them rather than invent a scalar:

1. **Rhythm** — the issues, meals, dog watches, Sunday: the day punctuated.
   (Losing the rhythm — cold food, stopped grog — hurts beyond the calories.)
2. **Variety** — treats, duff days, fresh fish, music, dancing.
3. **License** — the sanctioned blow-outs and the crew's own ceremonies;
   pressure released on schedule, at the captain's grant.
4. **Fairness** — the ration delivered full-measure; dilution-as-theft;
   the officer asymmetry; *explanation* (Bainbridge) as a real lever.
5. **Hope/connection** — mail, speaking ships, the homeward turn, pay-off.

Resource events from the parent doc land in these channels (short water →
fairness + rhythm; no antiscorbutics → hope, eventually bodies). This
document adds the *credit* side: music, dancing, ceremonies, treats.

### 5.4 The comfort economy

Gambling, tobacco and the auction want to be **event sources, not systems**:

- Gambling runs invisibly (as historically) and surfaces as incidents: a
  fight over debt, a man's kit staked and lost, tobacco hoards moving between
  hammocks. The captain's dial is ban-strictly / wink — with the log-study
  finding as the design rule: **you never see the gaming, only its wake.**
- Tobacco (already Tier 1) gains its real second job: the ship's small
  currency, the thing debts and favors are denominated in when grog is
  locked down.
- **The dead man's auction** is a scripted ceremony event with real economic
  and emotional effect — kit redistributed, wages book entries, the
  overbidding-as-charity choice offered to the player like everyone else.
  (Rare, and it should be.)
- **Private ventures** give each crew member one small parcel in the hold —
  characterization + a port-call hook, no commerce system.

### 5.5 Scenes and audio direction

Free wins the period hands us, all presentation-layer, none requiring
management gameplay:

- **audio**: singing-out cries at the windlass, a fiddle in the dog watch,
  a forebitter at night, the fife at the capstan — and **no shanty
  soundtrack**; a sailing game reflexively reaches for shanties and ours
  would be twenty years early. (The fiddle itself is an item aboard — whose
  it is, is a characterization choice for the crew round.)
- **scenes**: the grog tub at seven bells (the day's high point, twice);
  dog-watch dancing on the foredeck; the Saturday toast in the wardroom;
  Christmas with the sails already shortened; a cat on the hatch coaming.
- **wonder**: a bioluminescent wake on a dark night watch, logged in period
  journals with delight — our night ocean can do this someday.

### 5.6 What this adds to the resource roster: nothing

Deliberately. Spirits stays the single Tier-0 gauge (the custom dial changes
its *burn*, not its existence); tobacco stays Tier 1 with a stronger
justification; the captain's cabin wine and porter stay set dressing; the
fiddle is an item, not a resource. The research made the existing roster
richer without growing it — which is the outcome the parent doc's P1/P2
principles wanted.

## 6. Open questions for Ash

1. **The violence question, honestly.** The period's standard answer to
   drunkenness was the lash, and the *Beagle* opened her voyage flogging
   Christmas drunks. Does this game depict formal corporal punishment at
   all? If not (legitimate choice — tone, rating, taste), the honest
   alternative is already in our lineage: merchant custom, master's
   discretion, stoppage/watering and discharge-at-port as the severe end.
   The doc is written so either answer works, but the choice shapes the
   discipline system and should be made consciously.
2. **Drunkenness visibility**: is the crew's state shown (a gait, a voice,
   an S5 delay you can *feel* through the helm) or surfaced only through
   events? Recommendation: felt, not gauged — no "drunkenness meter" UI.
3. **Morale**: adopt the five-channel structure (§5.3) as the skeleton for
   the eventual crew round, or keep morale a single scalar with flavored
   events? The channels are more work and more truth.
4. **The custom dial** (§5.1): is alcohol policy a one-time captain-identity
   choice at voyage start, or a live dial the crew reacts to changing?
   (History says changing it *down* mid-voyage is the dangerous move —
   dilution-as-theft.)
5. **Ceremonies**: scripted set pieces (crossing the line, Christmas) —
   participate embodied, or watch as vignettes? Same probe as the parent
   doc's survey question, from the celebration side.

## 7. Relationship to the parent doc

- Confirms: no beer gauge; spirits Tier 0; tobacco Tier 1; officers' private
  stores as set dressing; spoilage/events-not-curves extends naturally to
  the social layer (gambling surfaces as events).
- Extends: the routine engine's 11:30/16:00 issue scenes now carry the
  documented emotional weight; the policy-dial table gains the alcohol
  custom; the event deck gains smuggling finds, gambling fights, the
  auction, Christmas.
- Defers: everything per-person (propensity, tolerance, the drunkard arc,
  whose fiddle) to the crew round, alongside the parent doc's morale
  deferral — this document is that round's source material.

## 8. Sources

Strongest single sources: DaCam, *"Wanton and Torturing Punishments":
Patterns of Discipline and Punishment in the Royal Navy 1783–1815* (PhD,
Hull 2009 — the ~5,000-event log sample, the units arithmetic, St Vincent's
rule, smuggling detail); Jamieson, "Tyranny of the Lash?" (*Northern
Mariner* 9:1, 1999 — three ships' complete punishment records incl. the
gambling absence); Brenckle's USS Constitution Museum monographs (the grog
tub procedure); Vernon's Order 349 (1740, primary); Banks's and Cook's
journals (crossing the line, Christmas, the Reading death); Darwin's diary
and FitzRoy's log (the *Beagle* Christmas floggings); Grose, *Classical
Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue* (1785 — able whackets); Dana, *Two Years
Before the Mast* (dead man's auction, temperance-ship sneer, event-issued
grog); Trotter, *An Essay on Drunkenness* (1804) with Edwards's 2012
appraisal in *Addiction*; the *Lydia* outfit (Vineyard Gazette); British
Tars (shanty dating, literacy, laundry of the era); Hektoen and RMG pieces
(dancing, *Bounty* fiddler); Rodger, *The Wooden World* and *Command of the
Ocean* (tolerated drunkenness, water-drinking revisionism, beer dates);
Wikipedia/Oxford Reference for dated institutions (rum ration reductions
1824/1850, US abolition 1862, sucking the monkey, widow's man, Trim, the
Well-Travelled Goat).

Dating cheat-sheet (keep OFF the 1790s ship): shanties and the word itself,
concertina/accordion, pictorial scrimshaw, macramé fancywork, "gam",

routine punishment, sippers/gulpers etiquette, Parry-style theatricals,
