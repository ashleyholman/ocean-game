# Pay, the wage book, and money aboard — concept notes

**Status: concept, third companion to `docs/provisioning/PROVISIONING_CONCEPT.md`**
(with `CREW_COMFORTS_CONCEPT.md`). Written 2026-08-14 from Ash's questions —
were wages paid daily, in what coin, kept where, tracked how, with what
paperwork, and did money circulate aboard — plus one research pass (sources
§7). Same contract: research and design thinking, nothing implemented, Ash
owns every call. Era flags matter here too: standardized pay forms and
shipping-office settlement are post-1835 institutions; in our 1780s–1800s
window the instruments are the articles, the master's books, and the
pay-off.

---

## 1. Ash's questions, answered short

- **Paid daily aboard?** No — and mostly not aboard at all. Pay was a *debt
  the ship owed you*, settled at the end. The Royal Navy paid in arrears
  (statutory theory: two months' wages every six months in home ports;
  practice: the mean delay in the Seven Years' War was **19.5 months**, six
  months' pay always withheld against desertion, foreign stations waited for
  home). Merchant crews signed articles at a monthly rate, took **one
  month's advance** at signing (customarily drunk before sailing), and
  settled the balance at pay-off — by law (1729) within thirty days of the
  ship's entry at a customs house.
- **Was there a coin store aboard?** Not for wages. The crew's pay existed
  as **ink in a book**; the ship carried modest physical money for *the
  ship's* needs: the captain's iron-bound chest with a few hundred dollars
  of specie for market purchases, and — the real spending power — **paper**:
  authority to draw bills of exchange (§3). "All the coins for the crew" is
  the one thing that was *never* aboard; that pile existed ashore, at the
  Navy Pay Office or the owner's counting-house, and met the crew only at
  pay-off.
- **Silver or gold?** The world's sea-currency was the **Spanish milled
  dollar** (silver, 8 reales — Dana still prices California in dollars and
  12½¢ reals in 1835). Britain's own silver was so short in the 1790s that
  the Bank of England **countermarked Spanish dollars with the King's head**
  (valued 4s 9d, from 1797 — the jingle: "Two Kings' heads and not worth a
  crown"). Gold guineas (21s) were officers' money. Copper for pennies.
- **The book, and where it lived**: on a vessel our size the master keeps
  the **articles** (the signed wage contract), a **wage/portage account**
  per man, and a **disbursement book** for ship's spending — in the cabin,
  with the chest. In the navy the equivalents were the muster book, pay
  book, and purser's slop and tobacco books. The below-decks plan already
  gives the captain's cabin its desk and lockers; this is what's in them.
- **Did crew get statements?** No. There was **no periodic paperwork to the
  man** — the reckoning came once, at pay-off. His protections were
  procedural: slops issued publicly *at the mast, in the captain's
  presence*; the (post-1795 RN) allotment system paying his family directly;
  and custom. The system's opacity was real and resented — forged-ticket
  wage theft is documented, and "Cheating Jack Tar" is a live topic in the
  scholarship.
- **Money flying around the ship?** Very little at sea — which is exactly
  why grog and tobacco were the internal currencies (companion doc). What
  silver a man had was pay-day residue or venture money. It came out at
  anchor: bumboats sold on credit against pay-day, peddlers and women came
  aboard on pay days to relieve the crew of coin. **Theft of a shipmate's
  money was the crime against the mess** — punished by running the gauntlet
  (a knotted-cat dozen, then the crew themselves), and social death after.
- **Did the captain have money?** Yes, in legally distinct piles: the
  *ship's* funds (owner's or Crown's — chest, bills, trade goods) and his
  *own* (private venture allowance, freight money on carried treasure, his
  personal credit). Expedition captains' real wallet was their commission:
  Cook paid for the *Endeavour*'s Batavia repairs by **drawing bills on the
  Navy Board** — any merchant house that trusted the paper would give coin
  or goods against it. La Pérouse carried piastres *and* ~700 presentation
  medals — money, diplomacy and souvenir in one object.
- **Ledger examples?** Real column sets and a worked reconstruction in §4.

## 2. The pay machine

### 2.1 Rates (the anchor numbers)

Per **lunar month** (the navy's 13-payday year), Royal Navy:

| Rating | to 1797 | after Spithead (1797) | 1806–15 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Able seaman | 24s | 29s 6d | 33s 6d |
| Ordinary | 19s | 23s 6d | 25s 6d |
| Landsman | ~18s | 21s 6d | 22s 6d |
| Lieutenant | — | £7 | £8 8s |
| Master (6th rate) | — | — | £7 7s |
| Boatswain/carpenter (6th rate) | — | — | £3 1s |

Deductions: 6d/month Greenwich Hospital (everyone), 1s "to the Chest",
and — until **1795** — the notorious **15s venereal "surgeon's fee"**,
docked at the pay table. (A man could dodge it only by concealing his
disease; a ready-made dilemma event for the crew round.)

Merchant wages are the context that explains everything: peacetime AB ~25s
a *calendar* month (Adam Smith: "a guinea to about seven-and-twenty
shillings"), **wartime 60–70s** — roughly triple, and ~4× navy base pay.
That gap is why the navy pressed men and withheld pay, and it prices our
expedition's offer: a survey schooner pays steady wages plus adventure, not
top dollar.

### 2.2 How pay actually flowed

- **RN**: entered in the muster book → wages accrued in the pay book → paid
  aboard at a home port when the Clerk of the Cheque's party rowed out with
  the coin (the ship's pay day announced in advance — creditors, wives, and
  bumboat traders converged on the ship for the settling of debts). Men
  transferred or discharged early got **pay tickets** — paper cashable
  later at the Pay Office in London, which a discount market bought at
  60–90% of face. In the Seven Years' War **over £9 million of unpaid naval
  wages circulated as this shadow paper**.
- From **1795**, a seaman could **allot** part of his pay directly to a
  named family member (AB: 5d/day), collected monthly at a customs or
  excise office — the period's remittance system, and a documented reason
  men gave for volunteering. The surviving registers' margin codes are
  their own grim poetry: **"DD after an entry stands for Discharged Dead."**
- **Merchant/our lineage**: articles signed at a monthly rate → month's
  advance → (rarely) cash advanced by the master at a foreign port, entered
  against wages → **portage bill** drawn up by the master at voyage's end:
  per man, the total earned, less advances, slops, and forfeitures →
  balance paid in coin at the owner's counting-house.

### 2.3 What it means for the game's fiction

The voyage is, financially, **one long held breath**: everyone aboard is
owed a growing number in a book they never see, everyone's small
consumptions nibble at it invisibly, and the whole thing resolves in one
scene at the home quay. That is a *narrative structure* — the pay-off is
the voyage's financial denouement, and the wage book is its quiet
antagonist or its honest friend depending on the captain the player chooses
to be.

## 3. The ship's money (as opposed to the crew's)

An expedition schooner's spending kit, assembled from the documented
practice (Cook/Batavia, La Pérouse, merchant custom):

1. **The iron-bound chest** in the captain's cabin — a working float of
   specie for markets and ports; order 200–500 Spanish dollars *(design
   extrapolation from precedent — no in-window manifest recovered; flagged)*.
   Keys: captain, and nobody else.
2. **The bill book** — the real spending power: authority to draw bills of
   exchange on the sponsoring Board or owners. Its limitation is the
   mechanic: **paper only works where paper is trusted.** A colonial
   treasury honors it, a merchant house discounts it, an island beach
   ignores it entirely.
3. **The trade-goods locker** — iron, nails, cloth, beads, mirrors, medals:
   the small change of non-monetized coasts. This is *exactly* the spec's
   §12 "one or two generic secured chests" of exchange items — the spec
   already drew this object; the research just explains it.
4. **The slop and tobacco stock** — the master-as-purser's investment, sold
   to the crew against wages (navy discipline: at the mast, weekly, in the
   captain's presence; capped ~5s/man/month, two months' pay for a new
   hand's kit).

So the answer to "where was the money kept" is threefold and spatial: the
crew's fortune was **ink in the cabin desk**, the ship's cash was **a chest
in the cabin**, and the ship's purchasing power on a wild coast was
**a locker of ironmongery** — all three already have homes in the
below-decks plan.

## 4. The books — real forms, and a worked example

### 4.1 The documented column sets

**RN muster book** (the format is stable for a century): number; name;
"Whence, and whether Pressed or Volunteer"; "Parish, Town, and County where
Born"; "Age when entered in this Ship"; "Qualities" (the rating); entry and
discharge dates; and the discharge codes — **D** (discharged), **DD**
(discharged dead), **R** (run, wages forfeit). Charge columns feed the pay
book: slops, beds, tobacco, purchases at dead men's auctions.

**Merchant portage bill** (mandated in substance by the 1729 UK and 1790 US
Acts; surviving examples): **name / quality / wages per month / time on
board / advance / cash received / charges / balance due.**

**Whaling accounts** (the fully-developed book-against-the-man, mature form
1840s): the lay (1/175 for Melville himself), against outfit debts, slop
chest, cash advances **at 25% interest** — documented voyages where a green
hand's three years came to ~$250, or to *debt*. Not our pay model, but the
cautionary extreme of the same bookkeeping.

### 4.2 A reconstructed wage account for our schooner

*Illustrative, not facsimile: the form, categories and wage/coin values are
documented; the specific prices are period-plausible approximations (no
in-window slop price schedule was recovered — flagged in §7).*

```text
                    WAGE ACCOUNT — Wm. HARDING, able seaman
        Entered 3 May, discharged 12 November: 6 months 9 days
                       at 40s per calendar month

  CREDITOR                                            £    s    d
    To wages, 6 mo. 9 da. at 40s ..................  12   12    0
    To short allowance money, 18 days at 4d .......        6    0
                                                    ---------------
                                                     12   18    0
  DEBTOR
    By advance at signing, one month ..............   2    0    0
    By slops: 1 pr shoes 5s, 1 shirt 4s 6d,
       1 blanket 6s ...............................       15    6
    By tobacco, 4 lb at 1s 6d .....................        6    0
    By 1 jacket, bought at auction of the effects
       of J. Cole, dec'd ..........................        8    0
    By cash at St Jago, 1 Spanish dollar ..........        4    9
                                                    ---------------
                                                      3   14    3

    BALANCE DUE AT PAY-OFF ........................   9    3    9
```

Every line is a game system already on the table: the advance (port round),
slops (Tier 1), tobacco (Tier 1), the auction (comforts doc §5.4), short
allowance money (parent doc §6.3), the dollar advanced ashore (liberty).
**The wage book is where all the other systems leave their receipts** — it
needs no mechanics of its own, only entries.

## 5. Design

### 5.1 What money is in this game: a ledger and a port interface

- **No money gauge, no economy sim.** At sea, money does nothing — that is
  the historical finding and the design gift. Wages accrue on the world
  clock as bookkeeping; the crew's balances are visible in the wage book
  (captain's cabin, an object you consult, like the mate's morning report);
  prices exist only inside port events.
- **The pay-off is the endgame accounting** — the one scene where the whole
  voyage's economy resolves per man: wages earned, minus what the book
  quietly collected, plus short-allowance money honored (or not), venture
  proceeds, the auction's small debts. For a management-leaning game this
  is the natural "voyage results screen" — except it is also diegetic,
  a table, a chest, and eight men waiting.
- **Port purchasing runs on the three instruments** (§3): coin (finite,
  universal), bills (unlimited-ish, trust-gated by port type), trade goods
  (the only currency on wild coasts). Port *type* thus prices the voyage
  plan: watering at a colonial port spends paper; watering at an island
  spends the exchange chests; and the chest of dollars is the buffer
  between. This slots straight into the terrain round's anchorage question
  without any market simulation.

### 5.2 The captain's honesty as a dial

The period system ran on opacity: no statements, purser markups (documented
US scale: 12–50% by category), forged-ticket wage theft, and the crew's
counter-institutions (public issue at the mast, the mutinies' demand for
16-oz pounds). Our captain *is* the purser — so the player chooses where on
that spectrum to stand: full-measure and shown workings (morale: fairness
channel), or the traditional quiet margins (money now, temper later). Same
shape as the grog custom dial — identity, not optimization.

### 5.3 Small mechanics the research hands us

- **The allotment**: at signing, some crew route pay home (5d/day to a
  named dependent). Backstory in one line each, and a standing entry in the
  book — the hope channel made financial.
- **The venture parcel** (comforts doc §5.4): each hand's private goods,
  bought cheap somewhere, sold dear somewhere else, *their* business — the
  period's one legitimate path from wages to wealth. Port flavor, zero
  simulation.
- **The auction** writes wage-book entries (already specced) — and the
  research adds the touching detail that the inventory-with-prices is the
  *historian's* window into a dead sailor's whole material life. Ours too:
  the game can show a man's life in eight lines and shillings.
- **Theft of coin** as the rare, grave social event (distinct from pilfered
  grog): historically the gauntlet and social death. Whether our game has
  any formal punishment scene ties to the comforts doc's open question #1.
- **The 15s dilemma** (pre-1795 fiction): a sick man conceals or reports.
  Surgeon-naturalist content, one event, real teeth.

### 5.4 Refused, with reasons

**Coin-counting UI or currency conversion play** (the countermarked-dollar
mess is flavor text, not a system); **a shipboard shop screen** (slops are
issued at the mast — a scene, not a menu); **prize money** (no armament, no
commerce raiding — the spec forbids the ship that would earn it; the
*dream* of prize money may live in crew talk); **interest-bearing debt
simulation** (the whalers' 25% is a cautionary flavor line, not our model);
**any market price simulation in ports** (port events quote prices; nothing
floats).

## 6. Open questions for Ash

1. **Does the player ever see money as money?** Recommendation: only three
   times — outfitting, port calls, pay-off. At sea the book is the only
   financial object. Confirms or kills §5.1.
2. **The honesty dial** (§5.2): worth building, or is our captain simply
   honest and the dial cut? (Cutting it is defensible — it trades a system
   for a simpler fiction.)
3. **Wages for an expedition**: fixed monthly wages (recommended — matches
   a government-adjacent survey and keeps arithmetic legible) vs any
   share/lay flavor?
4. **Is the wage book player-visible bookkeeping or ambient fiction?** i.e.
   do we simulate per-man balances from day one, or stage the pay-off
   narratively with coarse numbers? The reconstruction in §4.2 is cheap to
   maintain if entries come only from events — recommendation: simulate,
   it's a dozen integers.

## 7. Sources

Strongest: the Past & Present study of seamen's pay tickets (delay
statistics, the £9m shadow market); Spithead settlement documents (1797
rates and the purser's-pound abolition); Brenckle's slop-system monograph
(at-the-mast issue, caps, 5% commission); USS Constitution Museum "The
Pursers' Hustle" (US markup scales); the ADM 26 allotment registers
(1795 rates, the DD margin code); Merchant Seamen Act 1729 and the US 1790
Act (settlement law); Adam Smith and Ralph Davis (merchant wage rates);
Dana (dead man's auction verbatim; dollar-and-real prices ashore); Cook's
journal at Batavia (bills of exchange); Collection La Pérouse (piastres and
medals); British Numismatic Journal papers on the 1797 countermarked
dollars; HMS Richmond customs pages (gauntlet, ticket discounting);
Mystic Seaport and the *Maria Luz* portage bill (column sets — both later
than our window, flagged); New Bedford Whaling Museum and NHA (lay system).

Honest gaps: no in-window portage bill facsimile with filled amounts was
recoverable online (nearest: 1852 Mystic, 1872 *Maria Luz*; the c. 1800
brig *William* articles exist at LOC but the scan resisted transcription);
no primary source for the coins-in-the-hat pay-table image (kept out of the
doc); no in-window slop price schedule (why §4.2 is labeled a
reconstruction); Flinders'/Vancouver's per-port expenditure figures live in
TNA voyage accounts, not online.
