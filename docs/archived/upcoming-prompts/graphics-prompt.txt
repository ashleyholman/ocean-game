CORE VISUAL INTENT

The daytime experience should feel bright, clear, inviting, and beautiful—similar in emotional effect to being outside on an exceptional clear-weather ocean or tropical holiday.

However:

- the raft is in deep open ocean;
- do not turn the ocean into shallow turquoise lagoon water;
- do not use exaggerated postcard saturation;
- do not impose tropical midday lighting on physically incompatible high-latitude winter scenes;
- preserve real geographic, seasonal, and solar-angle differences.

Beauty must be robust across the supported environment controls rather than
belonging only to a small set of showcase coordinates and times. Every valid
combination of latitude, season, Sun angle, sea state and camera view should
lean into the visual qualities of that condition: calm water may be serene and
reflective, rough water powerful and sculptural, low polar light austere and
luminous, and night dark but richly legible. Do not force all conditions toward
one universal midday look.

Under suitable clear high-Sun conditions, the intended impression is:

- a recognisably rich blue sky;
- a deeper blue zenith;
- a lighter blue atmospheric horizon;
- clean, bright white clouds with visible internal shading;
- a clearly legible but naturally softened ocean horizon;
- a deep cobalt, navy, or ultramarine ocean;
- a spatially limited silver-white Sun-glitter path;
- an inviting, luminous appearance without broad grey washout.

At night, the intended impression is:

- genuine darkness and solitude;
- a deep navy to near-black sky and ocean;
- readable tonal separation rather than crushed featureless black;
- sharp real stars appearing progressively as twilight darkens;
- restrained Moon glow;
- a small warm pool of light around the raft;
- surrounding ocean that remains enormous and dark.

OBSERVED BASELINE PROBLEM

A representative current midday scene was observed at approximately:

- latitude: 4.33° south;
- longitude: 178.84° east;
- date: 24 January;
- local apparent solar time: approximately 12:53;
- wave amplitude: approximately 0.76 metres;
- wind: approximately 6 metres per second;
- camera distance: approximately 58 metres;
- sail lowered.

The current result has:

- a nearly uniform pale-grey or grey-blue sky;
- clouds and clear sky blending into a broad washed layer;
- a weak or indistinct blue zenith;
- a washed or low-contrast horizon;
- ocean reflection that is excessively broad, pale, and grey;
- insufficient deep-blue water-body colour;
- daylight that feels overcast despite low cloud coverage.

A second known problem occurs around sunset:

- when the low Sun is behind the camera and directly illuminates the sail,
  the sail receives too little warm directional colour;
- the cool directionless ambient contribution is currently strong enough to
  suppress the expected warm orange, red, or gold direct-light response;
- the sail therefore remains relatively neutral or cool instead of visibly
  belonging to the sunset illumination.

Do not solve these problems by merely:

- increasing global saturation;
- hard-coding the sky to blue;
- assigning one darker-blue RGB value to the ocean;
- reducing exposure globally;
- adding an orange full-screen colour grade at sunset;
- applying a broad grey or blue fog;
- making the Sun or Moon bloom larger.

Diagnose and correct the underlying rendering, lighting, tone-mapping, colour-management, atmosphere, material, and reflection relationships.

OCEAN OPTICAL MODEL

Treat the ocean as a combination of physically distinct components rather than
one fixed blue surface colour.

The model should explicitly or conceptually separate:

1. WATER-BODY RADIANCE

   Light that enters clear deep water, is spectrally absorbed and scattered,
   and emerges again.

2. FRESNEL SKY REFLECTION

   Reflection of the current procedural sky, increasingly dominant at grazing
   viewing angles.

3. DIRECT SOLAR GLITTER

   High-intensity reflection from wave facets whose normals align appropriately
   with the real Sun and camera.

4. FOAM OR WHITECAPS

   Approximately neutral bright material where already supported and
   physically justified.

- open-ocean optical profile.

- coastal turbidity;

SUN GLITTER

Requirements:

- a minimal secondary halo only for bright stars;
- analytic point rendering;
- another high-quality approach suitable for Three.js.

NIGHT SKY AND DARKNESS

Night must feel genuinely dark.

However, do not crush the entire image to featureless black.

The night system should preserve:

- a deep navy or near-black sky;
- subtle variation between zenith and horizon;
- a faint atmospheric or airglow floor if useful;
- visible real stars;
- Moonlight when applicable;
- enough local raft detail around the lamp;
- slight readable water movement near the raft or Moon reflection;
- truly dark distant ocean.

Avoid:

- lifted charcoal-grey blacks across the entire frame;
- a uniformly bright blue “day-for-night” treatment;
- pure black clipping that erases every form;
- excessive blue tint;
- exposure that turns the lamp or Moon into a giant white blob;
- a night sky as bright as twilight.

If gradient banding is visible, add subtle dithering or another restrained
solution. Do not add visibly noisy film grain.

EXPOSURE AND TONE MAPPING

Implement or recalibrate an HDR exposure strategy that works continuously from
midday to night.

Possible approaches include:

- a deterministic exposure curve driven primarily by Sun elevation and sky
  luminance;
- a restrained temporally adapted scene-luminance exposure;
- a hybrid of both.

Choose the simplest approach that produces stable, coherent results.

Requirements:

- no abrupt exposure jump at sunrise, sunset, or lamp activation;
- no distracting exposure pumping as waves or the sail move;
- midday sky retains blue rather than clipping broadly to pale grey;
- Sun highlights may clip locally without desaturating the entire sky;
- sunset retains both warm highlights and cool shadow colour;
- night remains dark;
- stars are not made visible in daylight merely by exposure;
- the raft lamp remains bright but retains a discernible source;
- manual debug exposure bias still works as an artistic override;
- exposure changes use presentation time, not accelerated world time, for
  temporal smoothing.

If automatic exposure is used, cap and smooth it carefully.

Do not use exposure to compensate for an incorrect direct-to-ambient light
ratio.
